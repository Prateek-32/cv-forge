A resume is a short, selective summary of your skills and experience, tailored to one job and usually one or two pages long. A CV, in its strict sense, is the complete record of your career, including publications, grants, teaching, clinical work or licences, and it has no fixed length. Which one you need depends on your field and on the country, because the word "CV" means different things in different places.

The two documents, defined

Resume (also written résumé): a marketing document. You choose what to include, lead with what is most relevant to the job, and leave out the rest. Its job is to get you an interview for a particular role.

CV (curriculum vitae, Latin for "course of life"): a record. In academic, medical and research settings it lists everything relevant to your professional standing, in full: every degree, position, publication, grant, conference talk, course taught and award. You add to it over a career rather than cutting it down for each application.

The confusion comes from the fact that in many countries, "CV" is simply the everyday word for what Americans call a resume.

How the words are used by country

India

In India, "resume" and "CV" are used almost interchangeably. A recruiter who says "send your CV" usually means a one- or two-page resume. You will also still see "biodata", an older format that included personal details such as date of birth and family information; for private-sector jobs it has largely given way to the resume. The strict long-form CV is used in Indian academia, medicine and research, and for applications to universities and fellowships.

The United Kingdom

In the UK, "CV" is the standard word for a job-application document, and "resume" is rarely used. A UK CV for a non-academic job is normally up to two pages: a short profile, experience, education and skills. UK academics and doctors use a longer CV, closer to the full record described above.

The United States and Canada

Here the distinction is sharpest. A resume is the document for almost every job in business, technology, government and the non-profit sector, typically one page early in a career and two for experienced people. A CV means the long academic or medical record, used for faculty posts, postdoctoral and research positions, graduate school, grants and medical training applications. Send a CV when someone asks for a resume and it will look unfocused; send a resume to an academic search committee and it will look incomplete.

The Gulf

In the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar and neighbouring countries, "CV" is the common term for a job-application document, usually around two pages. Employers there often expect personal details that Indian, UK and US employers do not, such as nationality, visa status and sometimes a photo. Check the employer's instructions. Which fields need the long-form CV

  • Academia. Faculty and lecturer posts, postdoctoral positions, PhD admissions, fellowships and grant applications. Publications, funding and teaching are the core of the document. See our academia and research page.
  • Medicine and healthcare. Doctors applying for residency, fellowship, specialist or consultant posts, and for jobs abroad, usually need a CV with registrations, clinical posts, procedures, audits, research and courses. See healthcare and medicine.
  • Scientific research. Research institutes, R&D laboratories and government science posts often ask for a full list of publications, patents and projects.
  • Some areas of law. Academic law posts, and applications where a record of publications, reported matters or appointments is expected.

Almost everyone else, including most engineers, analysts, finance, sales and marketing professionals and freshers, needs a resume, whatever the job posting calls it.

Length, sections and order

Resume

Length: one page for students, freshers and people in their first few years; up to two pages for experienced professionals.

Typical order:

  1. Name and contact details
  2. Short summary
  3. Experience (or, for freshers, education first, then projects and internships)
  4. Education
  5. Skills
  6. Certifications, and optionally achievements or languages

Each role gets three to five bullet points about results, not duties. Freshers should follow our resume format for freshers.

CV

Length: no fixed limit. Two to four pages is common early in an academic or medical career; senior academics' CVs can run much longer.

Typical order for an academic CV:

  1. Name and contact details, with ORCID or Google Scholar link
  2. Research interests or profile
  3. Education, including thesis title and supervisor
  4. Academic and research positions
  5. Publications, in a consistent citation style
  6. Grants, funding and fellowships
  7. Awards and honours
  8. Teaching and supervision
  9. Conference presentations and invited talks
  10. Service, memberships and referees

A medical CV follows a similar logic but puts registration and qualifications first, then clinical posts, procedures and skills, audits and quality improvement, research, teaching and courses such as life-support certifications. You can compare real layouts on our sample CVs page.

How to turn one into the other

From a CV to a resume

This is the common case for a researcher or doctor moving into industry, consulting or a corporate role.

  1. Write a three-line summary that states the role you want and the skills that transfer.
  2. Rename and reframe positions around outcomes: a postdoc becomes a role where you led projects, managed data, trained juniors and delivered results on deadlines.
  3. Replace the full publication list with a line such as "12 peer-reviewed publications" and, at most, two or three that matter to the new employer.
  4. Pull methods, tools and software out into a skills section.
  5. Drop conference lists, committee service and referees unless they are directly relevant.
  6. Cut to two pages at most.

From a resume to a CV

This happens when someone applies for a PhD, a teaching post or a fellowship, or a doctor applies abroad.

  1. Move education to the top and add thesis titles, supervisors and grades.
  2. Add complete sections for publications, presentations, teaching, grants and awards, even if each has only one or two entries.
  3. Use a consistent citation format for anything published.
  4. Expand positions to show research or clinical responsibility, not just commercial results.
  5. Add referees if the application asks for them.

Questions people ask

Is a CV the same as a resume in India?

In everyday use, mostly yes. Indian recruiters use the words interchangeably and usually want a one- or two-page resume. The long-form CV is used in academia, medicine and research.

How long should a CV be?

It depends on what "CV" means where you are applying. A UK or Gulf job CV is usually up to two pages. An academic or medical CV has no fixed limit and lengthens as your record grows.

Should I send a resume or a CV for a job in the US?

A resume, unless the role is academic, research or medical, or the employer specifically asks for a CV. American employers expect a focused one- or two-page resume for most jobs.

Do freshers need a CV or a resume?

A one-page resume, unless you are applying for a PhD, a research position or a fellowship, where a short academic CV listing projects, papers and presentations is expected.

Can one document work as both a resume and a CV?

Not well. A resume is cut to fit one job; a CV is complete by design. Keep a full CV as your master record and produce shorter, tailored resumes from it.
