Do you need a portfolio website? (and what to put on it)

By Fieldcraft · Updated 25 September 2026 · 7 min read

In short: A portfolio website helps anyone whose work is easier to show than to list. A good one is a single page: a headline, three to six problem-action-result projects, a short about section, credentials and contact details.

You need a portfolio website if your work is easier to show than to describe, or if your results need more room than a resume allows. For designers, developers, writers and film people it is close to expected. In finance, consulting, healthcare and teaching, a simple one-page site with a few well-written projects can still set you apart. It doesn't need to be elaborate. It needs to be current, quick to load and easy to find from your resume and LinkedIn.

Who benefits from a portfolio website

A portfolio does one job: it lets a recruiter or hiring manager see evidence before they speak to you. How much that matters depends on your field.

  • Designers (UX, product, graphic, interior, architecture): the work is the argument. Recruiters commonly ask for a portfolio link before anything else.
  • Software developers: a GitHub profile shows code, but not what it was for. A portfolio page explains two or three systems you built, the decisions you made and what changed as a result, with links to the repositories.
  • Data scientists and analysts: dashboards, notebooks and models are hard to judge from a bullet point. Write each one up with the business question, your method and the outcome.
  • Writers, journalists and content people: a page of your best clips, grouped by topic or format, saves an editor from searching for your byline.
  • Film, music and performance: a showreel near the top, then credits, festivals and roles.

Fields where a simple page still helps

Outside the creative and technical fields, most people assume a portfolio isn't for them. A plain one-page site can still work well:

  • Finance and banking: transactions listed by value, type, sector and year, the way banks record deals.
  • Consulting and operations: engagements written as problem, intervention and result, with clients anonymised.
  • Healthcare: registrations and licences first, then quality-improvement projects and publications.
  • Teaching: programmes you ran, resources you created and what changed for pupils.

You can see how the same one-page structure adapts to a banker, a physician, a teacher and a designer in our sample portfolios. If all your work is confidential and your CV already says everything, a strong LinkedIn profile may be enough. A portfolio adds most when it shows something the CV can't.

What a good one-page portfolio contains

Keep it to one scrolling page, so visitors reach your best work without clicking around.

  1. Headline. Your name, your role and what you are known for, in one line. "Backend engineer building payment systems in Go" tells a reader more than "Passionate problem-solver".
  2. Projects (3–6). Each with a title, your role, a short write-up and an image, chart or link where it helps.
  3. About. Three to five sentences: your experience, your focus and what you want next. Written in the first person, with no life story.
  4. Credentials. Degrees, licences, certifications, awards and publications. In regulated fields such as medicine, law and chartered accountancy, put these near the top.
  5. Contact. Your email written out in full, your LinkedIn and a link to download your resume. A contact form alone isn't enough, because some visitors won't trust it.

How many projects to show

Three to six is the usual range. Fewer than three can look thin. More than six and readers skim, and your weaker work drags down your stronger work.

  • Freshers: two or three strong projects (college, internship or self-initiated) are better than eight half-finished ones.
  • Mid-career: four or five, chosen for the jobs you want next rather than the ones you have done most.
  • Senior people: three to five headline pieces of work, with a link to a fuller list if you need one.

Put your strongest and most relevant project first. Many visitors never scroll past it.

Writing each project as problem, action, result

A screenshot with a title tells the reader what you made. A short case study tells them how you think. Use three parts:

  • Problem: the situation and why it mattered, in one or two sentences.
  • What you did: your part specifically. On team projects, say which parts were yours.
  • Result: what changed, with a figure if you have a real one. If you don't, describe the outcome plainly. Never invent a number.

An example: "Month-end close at a mid-sized manufacturer took eight working days. I mapped the reconciliation steps, automated three of them in Excel and Power Query, and rewrote the checklist. Close now takes five days, and the team has kept the new process."

Showing client work without breaking confidentiality

Much of the best work in consulting, finance, law, healthcare and agency design sits under a non-disclosure agreement or an employer policy. You can still write about it:

  • Check first. Read your contract or NDA. If you are unsure, ask your manager or the client for written permission.
  • Anonymise the client. "A large private-sector bank" or "a D2C skincare brand" is usually enough.
  • Use relative figures. "Cut processing time by about a third" gives away less than exact volumes or revenue.
  • Show the process, not the product. Sketches, redrawn diagrams, a sanitised workflow or a mock-up with dummy data can show your thinking without exposing anything real. Strip logos, names and internal URLs from screenshots.
  • Label concept work honestly. A self-initiated redesign is fine if you call it a concept, not client work.

Photo or no photo

A portfolio site isn't a CV, so the rules are looser. A clear, recent headshot can make the page feel personal, especially in client-facing roles, teaching and healthcare. It is optional. Many good portfolios, particularly in design and engineering, have no photo and let the work speak.

If you use one, keep it simple: good light, a plain background, your face clearly visible. Don't use group photos, cropped wedding pictures or heavy filters. Applying to the UK, the US, Canada or Australia? CVs there usually carry no photo, but your personal site can. Our guide to writing a CV for jobs abroad covers those conventions.

Free hosting, paying for hosting, and owning your domain

A one-page portfolio is a static site, and several well-known platforms host static sites free on their basic plans. That is enough for almost everyone. Paid hosting only makes sense if you need extras such as a shop.

What matters more is that the accounts are in your name. If a freelancer or agency hosts your site on their account, your site depends on them staying in business and answering your emails.

A free hosting address works, but it usually looks like yourname.some-platform.app. Your own domain (yourname.com or yourname.in) looks more established and stays with you if you ever move hosts. As a rough guide, a .com or .in domain generally costs somewhere between ₹500 and ₹1,500 a year, depending on the registrar. First-year offers are often cheaper than renewals, so check the renewal price before you buy. Register it in your own name and switch on auto-renew so it never lapses.

Linking it from your resume and LinkedIn

  • Resume: put the address in the header next to your email, phone and LinkedIn. Write it out as plain text (yourname.com) rather than hiding it behind a word like "Portfolio", because applicant tracking systems and printed copies don't keep links. There is more on this in our guide to ATS-friendly resumes.
  • LinkedIn: add the address to the website field in your contact info, and pin the site or one strong project to your Featured section.

Make sure your site, resume and LinkedIn agree on job titles, dates and figures. Recruiters notice when they don't.

Common mistakes

  • Showing everything you have ever made instead of your best three to six pieces.
  • Images with no explanation of the problem, your role or the result.
  • Leftover template text, "coming soon" sections or broken links.
  • Large uncompressed images that make the page slow on a phone.
  • No visible email address, or a contact form that doesn't work.
  • Letting it go stale: an old job title or a project from five years ago at the top.
  • Claiming sole credit for team work, or sharing confidential material without permission.

Questions people ask

Is a portfolio website worth it if I am not a designer?

Often, yes. In finance, consulting, healthcare and teaching, a simple one-page site listing your deals, engagements, projects or programmes can show scope and results that a two-page CV has to compress.

How many projects should a portfolio have?

Three to six. Freshers can show two or three strong ones. Put the most relevant project first, and cut anything that is weaker than the rest.

Can a fresher make a portfolio with no work experience?

Yes. College projects, internships, competition entries and self-initiated work all count, as long as you describe the problem, what you did and what came of it. Label concept work as a concept.

Do I need my own domain name?

No, but it helps. A free hosting address works. Your own domain looks more established and stays yours if you change hosts. It usually costs a few hundred to around fifteen hundred rupees a year, so check the renewal price.

Should I put a PDF portfolio or a website link on my resume?

A website link is usually easier: it opens on any phone, can be shared inside a company and is always up to date. Keep a PDF version ready for employers who ask for an attachment.
